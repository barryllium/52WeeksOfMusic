Over the past year, I've been asking people for recommendations for me to listen to. The Taxpayers came to me by way of my kid, Forest. I don't have a lot of history with punk, but there are a few bands over the years (and in the past year) that I really like - Siouxsie and the Banshees and the Mr. T Experience, to name a couple. After a few weeks of listening to them, at the very least I can say that my kid has good taste.

- "We Are the Hellhounds" (2007)
- *To Risk So Much For One Damn Meal* (2010)
- *"God, Forgive These Bastards": Songs From the Forgotten Life of Henry Turner* (2012)
- *Big Delusion Factory* (2016)
- *Circle Breaker* (2025)

One of the things I love about how I experience these bands is to witness their journey over time. I don't listen to everything, but I try to listen to the major touchstones along the way - and this lets me experience how a band changes, improves, and adapts from album to album. In the case of The Taxpayers, this really paid off for me as I grew to appreciate their music more and more as they evolved in the music they were making.

Before I go any further, let's do some expectation setting. No one is going to listen to The Taxpayers and say "now there are some pitch-perfect vocals". This is punk - I'm not even sure that's allowed. Almost every vocal performance is full of rough edges; and that's part of what makes the performance endearing and intriguing most of the time. Yes, sometimes there is too much screaming (I checked with past me, I wouldn't have liked it then either, so it's not *just* because I'm old), but the rest of the time, those vocal imperfections are like an additional instrument, and more-often-than-not feel complemented by the musical arrangement.

At this point, let's talk about the low point for me - which is the album *"God, Forgive These Bastards": Songs From the Forgotten Life of Henry Turner*. Much to my kid's chagrin, this was my least favorite of the albums I listened to. And it's not as if I hated it - it just didn't fare as well for me as the other three. That screaming I spoke of earlier? There are a few songs that were ruined by me because of it. Take "Goddamn These Hands of Mine" - the music is lit: the horns come in great, and the music has a strong drive to it. But the vocals just push me away - they feel too much at odds with everything else that is going on. Same goes for "Jimmy Bartlett's Teeth"; solid music (with borderline fantastic drums), but a vocal performance that lacks any restraint, thus negating the best things about it for me.

And yet... the album takes a huge turn for the better right after that song. "Hungry Dog in the Street", "The Business Man", "The Carriage Town Clinic", "I Love You Like an Alcoholic", and "Some Rotten Man" are five genuinely good songs to essentially close out this album (with the last two being two of my favorites of all their songs). All of these songs have great music, vocals that work (even if they are right up to the edge at times), and an excellent mix of instruments that lift each song.

In fact, let's take a step back to talk about the variety of instruments in all these albums. Many moons ago, while listening to The Rolling Stones, I mused "why don't more bands do these things?", in relation to how many random instruments would show up in their songs. The Taxpayers deliver tremendously here. Piano? Check. Harmonica? Check. Organ, cello, violin, accordion? ✅, ✅, ✅, ✅. THEY EVEN MADE ME LIKE THE BANJO IN A SONG. This is a big deal, people. I listen to artists like Mumford & Sons, and I start to get into a song - then the banjo kicks in and I check out (every song of theirs that I have heard would sound better to me if they swapped out the banjo for a drummer). But The Taxpayers freakin' did it in "Let the Wheels Turn Slowly" on *To Risk So Much For One Damn Meal*, against all odds. Other bands, take note.

And that's just the "extra" instruments. Across the board the bass and guitar playing does exactly what the songs need. During my second playthrough, I suddenly realized that the drums sound fantastic *constantly*. I would essentially be pointing out how strong the drums are in 2/3 of their songs if I were to list all the songs where the drums were excellent. And half the time, they were sneaky good; I'd be paying attention to some other layer - the vocals, horns, guitar, or something - and I would realize "wait - the drums are really great here", like I was taking them for granted.

Let's get back to the albums. From *To Risk So Much For One Damn Meal* all the way to *Circle Breaker*, you can see the growth. Parts of the music would "sneak up on me" more, coming in more subtly, creating even more of an impact when they reached their full force. The vocal performances got better, while still maintaining their imperfection and rawness - including harmonies showing up more often in the later albums. And for as much as there were songs I liked on the first two albums, *Big Delusion Factory* and *Circle Breaker* hit some home runs that set them apart from the earlier work. For as much as I liked the harmonica, the feel, and the buildup during the middle third of "Louisiana Hot Sauce Rainy Nights" or how "It Gets Worse Every Minute" knows exactly what type of song it is and nails it without trying to hit above its weight, those songs just don't compete with the best songs on the later albums. The biggest exception is "Some Rotten Man" - a truly authentic feeling song where the music swells and fades perfectly, accompanying the best vocal performance on *God, Forgive These Bastards...*.

For as much as the ending of *God, Forgive These Bastards...* won me over, *Big Delusion Factory* is where things hit a major uptick. After an... *interesting* first track, "Call Me Linda" and "Easy Money" crush it. The emotion behind the vocals (with the piano backing) in "Call Me Linda" is great, and then the tone of the song changes about two minutes in and the distortion adds a great layer onto the song, giving it even more of a presence. "Easy Money" continues musically right where "Call Me Linda" leaves off, with an awesome rhythm and pace. Every layer of the song - vocals, guitar, percussion, bass, piano - work well together. And the lyrics are perfect - it feels like the epitome of a punk song.

While I liked almost all of *Big Delusion Factory* (casting a side-eye at "Brain Drain (Tunnel of Love)"), the other song I'll single out is "Rarely at Least". The song is downright touching; the imperfect vocals bring emotion, and the simple piano arrangement works perfectly with the performance and lyrics. Halfway through, the music fills in just a little more (is that an organ behind the piano?), and I am completely drawn into the song.

But we aren't done yet - last up is *Circle Breaker*, which starts off strongly with "Circle Protector". The organ is key early in the song, and then there is a swell about 1.5 minutes into the song that raises the song up even more. Most importantly, this is where I first started noting that harmonies were happening and sounding good.

Right after that is "Evil Everywhere", an interesting song that I bring up because it's an example of something I found throughout every album. It's not even that I love this particular song - but it's that once the song comes together (which does take about 20 seconds), despite feeling chaotic and all over the place, somehow the musical arrangement works for me. Where there is discord, somehow The Taxpayers often find a way to create some cohesion out of it anyway, and I continually found that impressive.

*Circle Breaker* continues with song after song that I liked, almost always for different reasons. Regardless of how I felt about every song, across all the albums, at no point did I get bored with "sameness" from song to song. Whether it's the violins in "At War With the Dogcatchers" (and many other songs), the horns in "Empty Shed" (and, honestly, throughout their entire discography), the harmonica in "Constant Headlines", or the cello in "Naked Trees", every song delivered. The one that delivered most of all for me on this album, though, is "Nobody is a Lost Cause". It's emotional and vulnerable, with a simple presence that fills up the room with its lyrics and the delivery. Sometimes, all you need is a guitar and a story.

I don't hang out in punk music land often, but once again I'm glad I did. It didn't matter if it was a heavier song like "Weapon of God" or a calmer, simpler one like "Future Island", I enjoyed listening to a very talented group of people create a wide variety of music for me to enjoy. I don't say this about a lot of bands, but I think most anyone could listen to what I did this week and find at least a few songs they like, even if they didn't like everything. I don't say that lightly, and I mean it as high praise.

- Favorite album: I had this whole paragraph written in my head for days. I was going to talk about how *Circle Breaker* was The Taxpayers' best album, but my favorite was *Big Delusion Factory*. And it was really close to still being that during my second playthrough - but in the end, *Circle Breaker* is just a bit more consistent, and for as much as I'm still high on *Big Delusion Factory*, I think I enjoy *Circle Breaker* just a *little bit* more.
- Favorite song: This is a bit tougher. I could easily tell you my favorite song from each album, but there are some strong candidates across the board here. The two "finalists" are "Easy Money" and "At War With the Dogcatchers". As I sit here right now, despite the fact that "Easy Money" is a poster-child of a punk song to me, I have to give it to "At War With the Dogcatchers". The musical buildup to start the song - only to have the music mostly fade out for the vocals - is "chef's kiss" good. The bass, guitar, and percussion nail it. The horns are great throughout, the vocals are top notch (including the harmonies), and the song rises and falls perfectly from start to finish. The lyrics feel meaningful and are delivered in a way that deepens them. Oh, and the violins. They come in when they need to come in, and they leave right when they should (even if you don't want them to go). This song might be the confluence of everything I love about this band. And, they manage to use the modem dial-up sound in a way that works, so bonus points there.
