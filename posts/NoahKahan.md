Noah Kahan came to me by way of my youngest child, Sorin. A few months back he put *Stick Season* on, and I was a little intrigued, but I had only been able to listen to 3-4 songs before this week.

- *Busyhead* (2019)
- *Cape Elizabeth* (EP, 2020)
- *Stick Season (We'll All Be Here Forever)* (2023)
- *The Great Divide* (2026)

I don't need to spend much digital ink talking about *Cape Elizabeth* or *The Great Divide* - so I'll sum them up quickly. *Cape Elizabeth* had a good feel to it, and felt like something I'd put on in the background and enjoy passively - nothing truly stood out, but I also didn't dislike anything. *The Great Divide* had a few songs I appreciate, but they didn't bring much to the table that *Stick Season* didn't already bring, and did so in a way that didn't engage me as much as that previous album did. Again, not a bad album - it just didn't give me as much to talk about as the previous albums did.

Before talking about *Busyhead*, though, we need to talk about Mumford & Sons. I've not heard a lot of their songs, and yet I have a strong opinion about their work, *especially* their early albums. Let's take "The Cave" as a direct example. Every time that song starts, I think "oh, yeah, this is a good song". And I continue to enjoy it... until the banjo arrives in full force - at which point I want to turn the song off. The banjo and I are not friends - at least not in modern music. Dueling banjos? Sure, that's cool. Steve Martin doing crazy things? Yeah, I'm into that for about 5 minutes. But when you have a perfectly fine song, and you make the decision that "now the banjo will be the star" - that, my friend, is when we will have a disagreement. For this reason, with most Mumford & Sons songs, I have long had the opinion that if they would simply replace their banjo player with a drummer, I would like their music a whole lot more.

And that is what *Busyhead* brought to me. Complete vindication<sup>1</sup> that my thesis has been correct all these years. This album feels like what you would get if 1) Mumford & Sons had a drummer instead of a banjo player, and 2) Chris Martin was their lead singer on most of the songs. Right from the start, with "False Confidence", you've got a great moment when the bass and drums kick in after about a minute (made even better because they aren't a banjo). There's a grit and energy behind the song, and the vocal layering plays well with the music. The album continues with formidable songs, "Mess" giving me the impression of a "smoother" version of a song by The Menzingers - as if there is a similar DNA, but raised by different parents. "Hurt Somebody" lands so accessibly (especially lyrically) and Julia Michaels and Noah Kahan's voices enjoyably meld together. "Cynic", "Save Me", and "Carlo's Song" all stood out to me for similar reasons - and this is a trick that somehow Kahan managed to not make me tired of; he will start a song out with more of a mellow tone, and then a minute or two in (often for the chorus) some mix of percussion, keyboards, bass, guitars, and other instruments will jump into the song and gain my attention. It's a good trick, and he *almost* does it too often - but it still worked on me far more than it didn't. Listening to "Carlo's Song" was the first time his lyrics grabbed me, specifically this gem: "They tell me, 'Grief is just love letting go'. They say it like milk has been spilled on the floor. They say they don't know who I am anymore. Well, I just have never shown anger before". As someone who has spent much of his life trying to never be angry (and, frankly, pretty scared of anger overall), those lines spoke to me.

From start to finish, *Busyhead* is full of songs that all have a strong presence, with subtle grit and a musical identity that "knows who it is". The album flows well from beginning to end without a bad song in the mix.

And then we arrive at *Stick Season*<sup>2</sup>, where I listened to the extended version - and I'm glad I did. I had the chorus of "Northern Attitude" stuck in my head off-and-on for about two days - the way the music fills out after the laid-back verses really did it for me. Once we get to "Stick Season", though, we need to talk more about the banjo. This sonofabitch did it - he made me kinda like the banjo on a few songs. It's barely too much on "Stick Season", but he pulls it off in "All My Love". The bass and drums offset it just enough, juxtaposing its tone so it all works together. Later on, the restraint of the banjo - and, honestly, every part of the music - comes together to create an excellent mood in "Halloween" (the background vocals and the viola/violin elevate this song up to another level, too, making it one of my favorites of the week).

What *Stick Season* especially drove home for me, though, is that Kahan is a legit storyteller. I was starting to sit with this thought when I asked Sorin what he likes about him, and he said "he can really tell a story", and I knew even more that I was on to something. I had already gotten a taste of it back on *Busyhead* with "Hurt Somebody", but he really hit his stride on *Stick Season* with songs such as "Stick Season", "Homesick", "Growing Sideways", "Everywhere, Everything", and several others. I didn't grow up in Vermont, and there seem to be several differences between my life and Kahan's - but I could still feel his stories regardless of the size of the gap between our lives. His raw, unpolished vocal delivery (and solid use of his falsetto), combined with his lyrics, carries the emotion. In the spirit of Ray Charles and Otis Redding (but very different in practice and delivery), it's the imperfections in his voice that make the feelings stand out.

What else can I say about this album? He nails his trick from *Busyhead* repeatedly, with "Come Over", "Your Needs, My Needs", "Everywhere, Everything", and "Orange Juice" pulling off the "start off mellow, then kick the music up a notch later" scheme with aplomb. But that isn't all that he's doing - there are songs like "Strawberry Wine" and the openings of "Come Over"/"The View Between Villages" where the sparse arrangements were the high points.

While the banjo was heavily featured (shockingly in more satisfying ways than not), it's the other string instruments that star more often than not - the viola, violin, mandolin, fiddle, and 17 kinds of guitars.

And the lyrics - it's not only telling the story (though that's a big part of it), but also how the lines themselves are written. "Everywhere, Everything" made me stop what I was doing when I heard "It's been a long year and all of our book's pages are dog-eared". I could feel those words, that sense of no rest between one thing after another - yet each is significant in its own way. "Growing Sideways" has so many good lines that I could simply list half the lyrics here (don't worry, I won't) - but damn if "I'm terrified that I might never have met me" doesn't hit home as I continue to meet past and current versions of myself regularly.

But I can't finish talking about this album without discussing "Call Your Mom" - the biggest reason I'm glad I listened to the extended version of the album. Twenty-five years ago, I didn't even know "mental health" was a thing, and some days I feel like I'm still playing catch-up. Whether I'm trying to figure out how to handle too many feelings, too big feelings (or too many too big feelings), trying-and-failing to remind myself that the world will be OK if I don't do EVERYTHING, or even just caring about people that I profoundly, undyingly, and devastatingly care for as they fight through their own challenges, some days seem like a constant struggle. I'm grateful for all the people in my life that are there for me, and I'm so lucky to have people in my life that I get to know and care about. "Call Your Mom" came on when I was dealing with a "wonderful" cocktail of my own difficulties and caring deeply about people close to me as they were in the throes of their own. And you did it, Noah Kahan - I felt seen by your song. It's the story, the lyrics such as "All lights turned off can be turned on", that both gave me hope and a feeling of "none of us are alone".

A lot of what Noah Kahan brought to me felt like "what I needed" in the moment. A good deal of his music felt calm, in the midst of times when I was struggling to find calm within me. I got to feel, which I'm always grateful for. And, dammit, the guy even made me appreciate the banjo - a Sisyphean task if I've ever heard one.

- Favorite album: Here is where listening to things twice pays off. After my first complete playthrough, and after listening to *Busyhead* for the second time, *Busyhead* was the easy answer. I originally experienced the middle of *Stick Season* as having a bit of a "sameness" to it, whereas I liked every song on *Busyhead* both times through - and each song had its own identity. But then I went through *Stick Season* again: I heard more, I appreciated more, and I felt it more. The sameness faded away, and even though there are still some moments of "too much banjo", the parts and the whole bring *Stick Season (We'll All Be Here Forever)* to the top in the end.
- Favorite song: I knew the second I heard "Call Your Mom" for the first time it was my favorite song of the week, even though I hadn't finished the album yet (and hadn't even started *The Great Divide*). Through two listens, it still held up. His vocal performance melds wonderfully with the music, and the song has a number of subtle builds. The story is there and the lyrics are wonderful; "Call Your Mom" has left an impression on me.
- Your playlist:
  - *Busyhead*
  - "Anyway"
  - *Stick Season (We'll All Be Here Forever)*
  - "Willing and Able"
  - "Deny Deny Deny"

I'll be moving on to a band I first heard about years ago, but (to my knowledge) have never heard a single second of any song - Trampled by Turtles.
